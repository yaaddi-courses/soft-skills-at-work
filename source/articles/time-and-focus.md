# Managing time and focus

**Covers:** Eisenhower matrix, Time blocking, Pomodoro technique, Procrastination, Deep work

## Time is fixed, attention is not {#overview}
Everyone gets 24 hours a day, so the real question is where your attention goes. Many people feel busy all day and still miss what matters, because urgent small things crowd out important big ones. This article joins five tools: one for choosing what to do, two for scheduling it, one for getting started, and one for protecting concentration.

## Eisenhower matrix {#eisenhower-matrix}
The Eisenhower matrix sorts tasks by two questions: is it urgent, and is it important? That makes four boxes, each with a different action.

| | Urgent | Not urgent |
|---|---|---|
| **Important** | Do it now | Schedule it |
| **Not important** | Delegate it | Drop it |

The most valuable box is important but not urgent, such as planning, learning and exercise. Nothing forces you to do these things, so they slide unless you put them in a calendar. The matrix is named after President Dwight Eisenhower, who is said to have used the idea to organize his work.

## Time blocking {#time-blocking}
Time blocking means giving each task its own block on your calendar, instead of keeping a loose list. A to-do list says what, but not when, so you tend to pick the easy items. A calendar block turns a wish into a plan.

Estimate honestly, then add 25 percent, because most tasks take longer than expected. Leave gaps for the unplanned, and group similar tasks together, such as all email in two slots.

## Pomodoro technique {#pomodoro-technique}
The Pomodoro technique was devised by Francesco Cirillo in the late 1980s. You work for 25 minutes with full focus, then rest for 5. After four rounds, take a longer break of 15 to 30 minutes. The name comes from the tomato-shaped kitchen timer he used.

The short, fixed length makes a big task feel manageable, and the timer protects you from checking messages. If someone interrupts, write the request down and return to it in your break.

## Procrastination {#procrastination}
Procrastination is putting off a task even though you know that delay will cost you. Research suggests it is less about laziness or bad planning than about avoiding an unpleasant feeling, such as boredom, fear of failure or confusion about where to start.

Ways to break it: make the first step tiny, such as "open the document and write one sentence", use a five-minute start, remove the cause of the feeling by asking for help or clarifying the goal, and set a Pomodoro. Starting changes the feeling, and the task often gets easier once you have begun.

## Deep work {#deep-work}
Deep work is focused, distraction-free work on a demanding task. The professor Cal Newport used the phrase in his 2016 book. It contrasts with shallow work such as replying to routine messages. Deep work produces the things that are hard to copy, like a design, a plan or a piece of code.

Most people can do about three to four hours a day. Protect those hours: close the chat app, silence notifications, and book them as time blocks.

## A worked example {#example}
Sofia has these tasks on Monday morning. Sort them and then block her time.

| Task | Urgent? | Important? | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Client proposal due Wednesday | Soon | Yes | Do today, in a deep-work block |
| Answer routine emails | Yes | No | Delegate or batch |
| Plan next quarter | No | Yes | Schedule a block on Thursday |
| Reorganize old files | No | No | Drop |

Her calendar: 9:00 to 11:00 proposal (four Pomodoros of 25 minutes with 5-minute breaks, then a longer break), 11:30 to 12:00 email, Thursday 14:00 to 15:30 planning. Total deep work today: two hours.

## A common mistake {#mistake}
Letting urgent things decide the day. Notifications and requests feel urgent but are often not important. A second mistake is blocking every minute: a plan without gaps breaks at the first interruption.

## Key points {#key-points}
- The Eisenhower matrix sorts tasks into do, schedule, delegate and drop.
- Time blocking puts tasks on the calendar so they actually happen.
- The Pomodoro technique alternates 25 minutes of focus with short breaks.
- Procrastination is mostly about avoiding a feeling, so make the first step very small.
- Protect a few hours of deep work each day.
