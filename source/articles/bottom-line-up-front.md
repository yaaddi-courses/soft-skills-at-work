# Bottom line up front: writing for busy readers

**Covers:** BLUF

## Readers decide in seconds {#overview}
Most people read work messages in a hurry, between other tasks, often on a phone. They decide within a few lines whether the message matters and what they must do. If the main point is in the fifth paragraph, many will never reach it. Writing for such readers means putting the useful part first and the supporting detail after it.

## BLUF {#bluf}
BLUF stands for "bottom line up front". The idea comes from military writing, where a reader may have to act on a message in minutes. You open with the conclusion, decision or request, and then add the background in order of importance.

The bottom line answers one question: what do I want this reader to know or do? It is often a single sentence. After that you give the reasons, the facts and any options. A reader who stops after the first two lines still has the key point, and a reader who wants more finds it just below.

The method works in speech too. When a manager asks "How is the project going?", begin with "On track, with one risk" and then explain.

## Writing a good bottom line {#writing}
A strong first line contains an action or a decision, a deadline if there is one, and who is responsible. Compare:

- Weak: "Following up on last week's discussion about the vendor contract, I wanted to share some thoughts."
- Strong: "Please approve the vendor contract by Thursday, because the price rises on Friday."

Put the question, not the history, at the top. Use a clear subject line as an extra bottom line, for example "Decision needed by Thursday: vendor contract". Keep paragraphs short, and use a list when you have several options.

## A worked example {#example}
Here is the same message written two ways. Count the words before the reader learns what to do.

Before (the reader waits until the end):

> Hi Maria. Last month we reviewed our software licences and found that three of them overlap. After checking usage, it seems that the design tool is used by only four people. The renewal date is next Monday. Given all this, I would suggest we cancel the extra licences, which would save about 1,200 dollars a year. Let me know if you agree.

After (BLUF):

> Hi Maria. Can you approve cancelling the unused design tool licences by Friday? It saves about 1,200 dollars a year. The renewal is next Monday, and only four people use the tool.

Compare the two versions:

| | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| Total words | 62 | 32 |
| Request begins at word | about 43 | 5 |
| Date and benefit visible in the first line | No | Yes |

The second version is 30 words shorter, about 48 percent less, and the reader knows what to do by the fifth word.

## A common mistake {#mistake}
Using BLUF for bad news without care. Opening with "Your proposal is rejected" can feel cold. You can still lead with the result and soften it with the reason straight away: "We cannot fund the proposal this quarter, but I would like to help you reapply in January." A second mistake is a bottom line that is vague, such as "I have some updates". A reader still has to hunt for the point.

## Key points {#key-points}
- Put the conclusion or request first, then the details.
- A good bottom line includes the action, the deadline and who must act.
- BLUF also works in meetings and conversations, not only in email.
- Keep the tone kind when the news is bad, but still lead with the result.
