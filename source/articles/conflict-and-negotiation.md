# Conflict and negotiation

**Covers:** Thomas-Kilmann modes, Interest-based approach, BATNA, Anchoring

## Two sides of the same table {#overview}
Conflict and negotiation look different, but they share a problem: two people want something and cannot both have all of it. In conflict the issue is often a disagreement about work. In negotiation it is a deal, such as a salary or a deadline. Four ideas help in both cases: knowing your usual style, looking beneath demands for needs, knowing your fallback, and watching out for the first number. Let's take them one by one.

## Thomas-Kilmann modes {#thomas-kilmann-modes}
When you disagree with someone, what do you usually do? Kenneth Thomas and Ralph Kilmann described five typical responses. They arrange them on two scales: how much you push for your own goals, and how much you care about the other person's.

- **Competing:** high on your goals, low on theirs. You push to win.
- **Accommodating:** low on yours, high on theirs. You give way.
- **Avoiding:** low on both. You postpone or sidestep.
- **Compromising:** medium on both. Each side gives up part of what it wants.
- **Collaborating:** high on both. You look for a solution that meets both goals.

No mode is always right. Competing suits an emergency, avoiding suits a minor issue or a heated moment, and accommodating suits a matter that matters more to the other person. Collaborating takes the most time, so it pays off on important issues. Most people have a favourite mode that they overuse, so noticing yours is the first step.

## Interest-based approach {#interest-based-approach}
The interest-based approach resolves a conflict by looking at what each side needs and why, instead of the positions they first announce. The idea comes from the book "Getting to Yes" by Roger Fisher and William Ury.

A position is "I want the corner office". The interest behind it might be quiet, status or natural light. Once the interests are known, many options open that satisfy both, such as a quiet room with a window. Ask "why is that important to you?" and listen to the answer. Separate the people from the problem, and agree on fair standards where you can.

## BATNA {#batna}
Before you negotiate, ask yourself: what will I do if the talks fail? The answer is your BATNA, which stands for best alternative to a negotiated agreement. Fisher and Ury introduced this idea too. A strong BATNA gives you the freedom to say no, and a weak one pushes you toward a bad deal.

Work out yours before you start. If you are negotiating a salary, it might be a second job offer, or staying in your current job with its current pay. Improve it if you can, for instance by applying elsewhere. Also think about the other side's BATNA, because it shows how much room they have.

## Anchoring {#anchoring}
The first number or idea mentioned has a strong pull on everything that follows. That pull is called anchoring. Psychologists Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman showed it in 1974: people's estimates moved toward a random number they had seen moments earlier.

In a negotiation, the first offer sets the range. If the seller asks 20,000 and the buyer answers 10,000, the final price will probably land near 15,000, the middle. If you can make the first offer, make a considered one. If the other side does, do not adjust from their number: restate your own reasoning and your own figure.

## A worked example {#example}
Lena wants a salary of 60,000. The employer first says 52,000. Let's follow her.

1. Lena's BATNA is her current job at 54,000 plus a pending interview elsewhere. So she can refuse anything under 54,000.
2. The 52,000 is an anchor. Lena does not respond with 56,000, which would accept the anchor's range. She restates her research: "Similar roles in this city pay 58,000 to 64,000, so I am asking for 60,000."
3. She asks about interests: "What matters most for this role?" The employer says the budget is fixed but they can offer extra leave.
4. They agree on 57,000 and five extra days of leave, which is a collaborative result.

Compare with the compromise mode: splitting 52,000 and 60,000 gives 56,000 and no extra leave. The interest-based talk produced a better deal for both.

## A common mistake {#mistake}
A common slip is treating a single mode as the best one. A person who always collaborates can waste time on small matters, and a person who always competes damages relationships.

A second slip is entering a negotiation without a BATNA, which leaves you accepting whatever is offered.

## Key points {#key-points}
- The five Thomas-Kilmann modes are competing, accommodating, avoiding, compromising and collaborating, and each fits some situations.
- Look at interests, not positions, to find options both sides can accept.
- Know your BATNA before negotiating, and estimate theirs.
- The first number acts as an anchor, so choose your first offer with care.
