# Feedback that people can use

**Covers:** SBI model, SBI, Feedforward, Growth mindset

## Why feedback often fails {#overview}
Feedback is meant to help someone improve, yet it often leaves people hurt or confused. The usual reasons are that it is too vague ("be more professional"), too personal ("you are careless") or too late. Three ideas make it more useful: a clear structure, a forward focus, and a mindset in which the receiver can actually take it in.

## SBI model {#sbi-model}
The SBI model comes from the Center for Creative Leadership. It has three parts that keep feedback specific and free of judgment.

1. **Situation:** when and where it happened.
2. **Behavior:** what you saw or heard, described like a camera would record it.
3. **Impact:** the effect on you, the team or the work.

A sentence in this shape sounds like: "In yesterday's client call (situation), you interrupted Priya twice (behavior), and she stopped sharing her numbers (impact)." Nothing in it guesses at motives or labels the person.

## SBI {#sbi}
People often just say "SBI" as a shorthand: "Let me give you some SBI on the presentation." When you hear it, the speaker intends the same three steps. If you want feedback from a colleague, you can ask for it in this form: "Could you give me the situation, behavior and impact?"

## Feedforward {#feedforward}
Feedforward is feedback that points to what someone can do better next time, not what went wrong before. The coach Marshall Goldsmith popularized the term. The past cannot be changed, so talking about it can make people defensive. Suggestions for the future feel like help.

You can add it to the end of an SBI message: "Next time, could you let Priya finish and then add your point?" Another way is to ask the person: "What would you like to do differently in the next call?" People are more likely to follow advice they have helped to write.

## Growth mindset {#growth-mindset}
A growth mindset is the belief that abilities can improve with effort, good strategies and feedback. The psychologist Carol Dweck contrasted it with a fixed mindset, which treats talent as something you either have or do not have.

It matters for feedback because people with a fixed mindset hear criticism as a verdict on who they are, so they argue or hide. People with a growth mindset hear it as information. As a giver, you can encourage this by praising effort and strategy ("you tested three options") instead of talent ("you are a natural"). As a receiver, you can ask yourself: "What can I learn from this?"

## A worked example {#example}
A manager wants to give feedback to Omar, whose weekly report keeps arriving late.

| Part | Message |
|---|---|
| Situation | "On the last three Fridays..." |
| Behavior | "...the report arrived after 5 p.m., when the deadline is noon." |
| Impact | "That meant I could not send the summary to the director on time, and she asked me why." |
| Feedforward | "What would help you send it by noon next week? I can move the data export to Thursday if that helps." |

Notice what is left out: "you are unreliable", "you do not care", "you always". Only what happened and what it caused. Omar can respond to facts. He cannot respond to an accusation.

## A common mistake {#mistake}
Burying criticism inside praise, so that the receiver hears only the compliments. Another mistake is waiting weeks. Feedback works best within a day or two, when both people remember the event. Praise works the same way: specific and quick.

## Key points {#key-points}
- Use the SBI model: describe the situation, the observed behavior and its impact.
- Feedforward looks ahead and asks what to do next time.
- A growth mindset treats feedback as information that helps you improve.
- Be specific, be quick, and leave out labels and guesses about motives.
