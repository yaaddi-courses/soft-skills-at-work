# Listening so that people feel heard

**Covers:** Active listening, Paraphrasing, Open-ended question

## The skill behind every other skill {#overview}
Most workplace problems have a communication story somewhere in them: a request that was misunderstood, a worry that was never voiced, a decision made without a key fact. Good listening prevents many of these. It is also one of the cheapest skills to practice, because it costs nothing except attention.

This article covers three tools that work together: listening fully, checking that you understood, and asking questions that invite a full answer.

## Active listening {#active-listening}
Active listening means giving your whole attention to the speaker and showing that you follow. Ordinary listening is often a pause between your turns to speak. In active listening, you are not preparing your reply while the other person talks.

Habits that help:
- Put your phone and laptop aside and face the speaker.
- Use small signals: a nod, "I see", an open posture.
- Let silences last a moment. People often add the most important detail after a pause.
- Notice feelings as well as facts, such as worry or excitement in the voice.
- Hold your opinion until they have finished.

## Paraphrasing {#paraphrasing}
Paraphrasing is repeating what you heard in your own words, so that the speaker can confirm or correct it. It is not parrot-like repetition. Compare "You said the deadline is Friday" with "So the client needs the draft by Friday, and the design can come later?" The second version shows you processed the meaning.

Useful openers are "So you are saying...", "If I understand correctly...", and "It sounds like...". Do it at natural breaks, and keep it short. If the speaker says "not exactly", you have found a misunderstanding early, when it costs almost nothing to fix.

## Open-ended question {#open-ended-question}
An open-ended question cannot be answered with yes or no. It starts with words such as what, how or tell me about. It invites the person to explain in their own words, which gives you more information and makes them feel respected.

A closed question like "Did the meeting go well?" gets one word. An open one like "What stood out to you in the meeting?" gets a story. Closed questions still have a place, for example to confirm a fact quickly: "Is the report finished?"

## A worked example {#example}
A teammate says: "I'm not sure the new schedule works for me."

| Step | What you say | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | (Stop typing and look up) | Shows full attention |
| 2 | "What part of it worries you?" | Open-ended question |
| 3 | They explain: school pick-up clashes with the 9 a.m. start | You learn the real issue |
| 4 | "So the 9 a.m. start is the problem, and the rest is fine?" | Paraphrase to check |
| 5 | "Yes, 9:30 would solve it." | A concrete fix, found in one minute |

Without steps 2 and 4, you might have changed the whole schedule, or guessed wrong about what the problem was.

## A common mistake {#mistake}
Giving advice too early. When someone describes a problem, the urge to say "you should..." is strong, but advice offered before you understand the issue often misses it. First ask, then check with a paraphrase, then offer help if they want it. A second mistake is asking questions that hide a opinion, such as "Don't you think that is a bad idea?" That is a closed question in disguise.

## Key points {#key-points}
- Active listening means full attention, with small signals that you are following.
- Paraphrasing checks your understanding and catches errors early.
- Open-ended questions invite explanation. Use closed questions to confirm facts.
- Listen and ask before you advise.
