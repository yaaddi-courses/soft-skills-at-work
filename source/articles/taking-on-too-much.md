# Taking on too much: scope creep and burnout

**Covers:** Scope creep, Burnout

## How overload builds up {#overview}
Overload rarely arrives in one dramatic moment. It builds from small yeses: one more request, one more feature, one more evening of work. Each addition seems reasonable on its own. In this article we follow two linked problems, one in the work itself and one in the person doing it.

## Scope creep {#scope-creep}
Picture a project agreed at a fixed size. Then the requests start: "Could you also add a filter?", "Can we change the colours?", "While you are there, fix this other page". Scope creep is the slow growth of a project beyond what was first agreed, without matching extra time, money or people. It rarely comes from one big demand. It comes from small additions.

Each request may be sensible, but together they push the deadline and the budget. The team ends up late, tired and apologizing for work the client did not originally ask for.

The defences are plain. Write down what is included and what is not at the start. When a new request arrives, estimate its cost, and then offer a choice: add it and move the date, add it and drop something else, or put it on a list for the next round. Give your answer in writing so that everyone remembers the agreement.

## Burnout {#burnout}
The second problem sits in the person. Burnout is a state of exhaustion caused by long-term stress at work that has not been managed. The World Health Organization describes it as having three parts: feeling drained of energy, feeling distant or cynical about the job, and being less effective.

It differs from ordinary tiredness. A good weekend fixes tiredness, but burnout remains after rest. Early signs include dreading Mondays, irritability, trouble concentrating, headaches or poor sleep, and a feeling that nothing you do matters. Causes are usually in the work: too much load, too little control, unfair treatment, unclear roles and lack of recognition.

What helps: talk to your manager about the load, set boundaries on after-hours messages, take real breaks and holidays, protect sleep and exercise, and seek professional help if the symptoms persist. Organizations carry responsibility too, since the best fix is to change the work, not only to teach people to cope.

## A worked example {#example}
A web project is agreed at 100 hours over 5 weeks. Let's see how small additions add up.

| Request | Extra hours |
|---|---|
| Change button colours | 2 |
| Add a search filter | 12 |
| Rework the contact page | 8 |
| "One more report" | 10 |
| Total extra | 32 |

The project is now 100 + 32 = 132 hours, which is 32 percent more than agreed. At 20 hours a week, the five weeks become 132 divided by 20, which is 6.6 weeks, nearly seven. If the team does not move the deadline, they must work 32 divided by 5, about 6.4 extra hours each week, to stay on time. Over several projects like this, a person is on the path to burnout.

The better move is to answer the filter request with: "That would add about 12 hours. I can add it and move delivery to week 6, or swap it for the report. Which do you prefer?" The work stays honest, and the client chooses.

## A common mistake {#mistake}
A common slip is saying yes to avoid an awkward moment, then paying for it later.

A second slip is waiting until you are exhausted to raise the problem. Early, specific messages about load are easier for a manager to act on than a late one that sounds like a crisis.

## Key points {#key-points}
- Scope creep is the quiet growth of a project through small, unpaid additions.
- Write down the scope, price each new request, and offer trade-offs.
- Burnout is long-term work stress that causes exhaustion, cynicism and lower effectiveness.
- Rest alone does not fix burnout. The work itself usually needs to change.
